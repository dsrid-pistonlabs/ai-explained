## What happened?

In January 2024, an employee at a Hong Kong company reportedly transferred HK$200 million after a video call with people posing as senior company officers. Police said the call appeared to use deepfake video and voices. The employee reportedly made 15 transfers before checking with head office and realising it was a scam. [The Guardian’s report](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2024/feb/05/hong-kong-company-deepfake-video-conference-call-scam) summarises what police said at the time.

## So what?

Video and voice can be copied convincingly enough to support a scam. A familiar face on screen is no longer proof that a request is genuine—especially when money, passwords, or confidential information are involved.

## What can you do?

- Verify unusual payment requests by calling the person back on a number you already trust.
- Follow your organisation’s normal approval process, even if the request seems urgent or comes from a senior person.
- Treat secrecy or pressure to bypass checks as a warning sign. Pause and ask someone else to verify.

These checks help whether the impersonation uses AI or a more ordinary scam.
